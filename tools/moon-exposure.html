Moon exposure is the shutter speed that correctly exposes the moon's surface — and it follows a fixed rule, because the moon is simply a sunlit subject at a known brightness. This calculator applies the Looney 11 rule with phase adjustments to give you the exact settings.

How to use this moon exposure calculator

Pick the moon's phase and your ISO, and the calculator returns the shutter speed at your chosen aperture. The math: a full moon is a sunlit surface worth about EV 13.6 — the Looney 11 baseline (f/11 at 1/ISO). Each phase step dims the lit surface by roughly one stop. Meter with spot mode directly on the moon if your camera supports it; the Looney 11 numbers are the failsafe when the meter lies (it will, because the sky around the moon is black).

Worked example

A full moon at ISO 100, f/11: Looney 11 gives 1/100 s. Shoot the same moon at ISO 400 and you'd use 1/400 s (or f/22 at 1/100 s). A quarter moon is two stops dimmer: at ISO 100, f/8, that's about 1/50 s. Handheld is fine for the moon — at these shutter speeds camera shake is rarely the problem; a tripod matters when you zoom to 300 mm+ or shoot the moon with a landscape.

Moon exposure FAQ

What is the Looney 11 rule for moon photography?

Looney 11 (the moon's equivalent of sunny 16): full moon at f/11, shutter 1/ISO. At ISO 100 that's 1/100 s at f/11 — the moon is a sunlit subject, so it needs a daylight exposure, not a night exposure.

How do I expose the moon at different phases?

Start from full moon (f/11 at 1/ISO). Add one stop for gibbous, two for quarter, three to four for a crescent — the lit fraction of the face gets dimmer as it narrows.

Why is my moon overexposed when I shoot at night?

Your camera meters the dark sky and stretches the exposure — but the moon itself is sunlit and bright. Meter directly off the moon (spot mode) or use the Looney 11 settings and let the sky go black.

Can I photograph the moon and landscape in one shot?

The moon is about 12 stops brighter than a blue-hour landscape, so one exposure can't hold both. Shoot two frames and blend, or catch a crescent moon in twilight, which is much closer in brightness.
