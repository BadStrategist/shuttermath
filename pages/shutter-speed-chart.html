Shutter Speed Chart

The shutter speed that freezes your subject depends on how fast it moves across the frame — not just its real speed. These are proven starting points; check sharpness at 100% and adjust.

Freeze or blur: recommended speeds

SubjectRecommendedNotes
Birds in flight1/2000 – 1/4000 sFast wings need the fastest speeds; use burst + tracking
Motorsport / racing1/1000 – 1/2000 sOr 1/60 – 1/125 s for a panning shot with blurred background
Ball sports (tennis, soccer)1/1000 – 1/2000 sBall in frame = go faster
Kids & pets playing1/500 – 1/1000 sUnpredictable movement; fast enough to save candid shots
Street / documentary1/250 sFreezes walking subjects; wide enough for available light
Portraits1/125 – 1/250 sPeople pose; this beats camera shake with stabilization
Handheld landscapes1/focal length (or 2× for APS-C)With stabilization you can go 2–4 stops slower
Macro (handheld)1/200 s or fasterMagnification amplifies shake — see the macro guide

City lights / night2 – 30 sTripod; bulb mode for longer

Video: the 180° rule

For video, shutter is set by the 180° rule: 1/(2 × frame rate). The full math is in the shutter angle calculator:

Frame rate180° shutter

Handheld rule of thumb

The classic anti-shake rule: shutter ≥ 1 ÷ focal length (35 mm equivalent). A 200 mm lens needs ≥ 1/200 s; a 24 mm can go as slow as 1/24 s. Modern image stabilization buys you 2–4 extra stops — but only for your shake, not subject movement.

Shutter speed FAQ

Why do I need a faster shutter for a flying bird than a running dog?

It's about speed across the frame, which depends on distance and lens magnification too. A bird at 10 m with a 400 mm lens crosses the frame much faster than a dog at 5 m with a 35 mm lens — hence 1/2000+.

Is a slower shutter ever better?

Yes — when you want motion blur: panning with a car (1/60–1/125 s), silky water (1/2–2 s), light trails (seconds), or intentional camera movement. Blur tells the story of motion; freezing erases it.

Does image stabilization let me ignore the rule?

For static subjects, yes — you can go 2–4 stops slower handheld. For moving subjects, no: stabilization counters your shake, not the subject's motion. Keep the subject speed, not the rule.
