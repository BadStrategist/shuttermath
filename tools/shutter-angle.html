Free Shutter Angle Calculator

Shutter angle expresses shutter speed as a fraction of the frame interval, with 360° being one full frame. In video, shutter speed is described as an angle. The cinematic standard — the 180° rule — sets the shutter to 1/(2 × frame rate): 1/48 s at 24 fps, 1/60 s at 30 fps, 1/120 s at 60 fps. That's the natural motion blur your eye expects.

Worked example

You're shooting 24 fps and want the standard cinematic look: enter 180°, and the calculator returns 1/48 s — the classic rule (shutter = 1/(2×fps)). Switch to 60 fps for slow motion and it becomes 1/120 s. Now the creative choices: 360° (1/24 s) adds motion blur for dreamy pans, while 90° (1/96 s) makes action crisper and more staccato. Videographers memorize 180° because it sits at the sweet spot — enough blur for smooth motion, enough edge for detail. The calculator removes the mental math when you're juggling frame rates, resolutions and ND filters on set.

Shutter angle FAQ

Why 180 degrees?

Shutter angle describes how long the shutter stays open per frame, as a fraction of a full rotation. At 180° it's open half the frame time, giving motion blur that matches how the eye tracks movement — too open (360°) blurs, too closed (45°) stutters.

What shutter speed for slow motion?

Same rule: 1/(2 × fps). At 120 fps that's 1/240 s. Many cameras auto-set this when you select 180° in video mode — check your menu.

Does the 180° rule apply to stills?

No — stills use shutter speed directly (1/focal length or faster for handheld). The angle system is a video/cinema convention, though the motion-blur logic is the same.

Shutter speed by frame rate and shutter angle

Short answer: the 180° shutter rule sets exposure time to half the frame interval, so 24 fps gives 1/48 s, 30 fps gives 1/60 s and 60 fps gives 1/120 s. Multiply the frame rate by 360 and divide by the shutter angle to get the exact speed: 24 × 360 / 180 = 48.

Why 180° is the default

A 180° shutter gives motion blur that matches how the eye reads movement at 24 fps. Narrow the angle (90°, 45°) and you get crisper frames that survive slow motion and look like Saving Private Ryan; open it to 360° and motion smears, which suits dream sequences and low light.

Frame rate and shutter angle together decide whether lights flicker. At 50 Hz mains, shoot 25 or 50 fps; at 60 Hz, shoot 30 or 60 fps. On mixed or unknown lighting, a narrow angle lets you keep 24 fps without banding.
