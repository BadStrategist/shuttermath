Why video needs ND filters

Stills photographers "fix" bright scenes with shutter speed and aperture. Videographers can't:

  • Shutter is locked by the 180° rule (1/48 s at 24 fps) — the motion blur is the look.
  • Aperture is limited — stop past f/8–f/11 and diffraction softens the image; plus you often want f/2.8 for shallow depth of field.
  • ISO bottom is limited — most cameras go to ISO 100, and going "below base" is impossible.

With a fixed 1/48 s shutter at base ISO in sunlight, the only thing left between you and overexposure is glass in front of the lens. A 6–10 stop variable ND covers nearly every daylight situation.

Video ND FAQ

What ND strength should a videographer buy first?

A variable ND 2–5 or 6–9 stops covers daylight at typical apertures. Pair it with a fixed ND64 (6 stops) for the rare case you need repeatable settings. Avoid cheap variables — they cause X-pattern color cast at the dark end.

Does the calculator work for log / flat profiles?

Mostly — log profiles change the exposure curve but not the light math. You may find you need ⅓–⅔ stop more ND when exposing-to-the-right for log; check the histogram and add a third-stop if the highlights clip.

What if my base exposure is between two filters?

Split the difference: open the aperture ⅓–½ stop, drop ISO one step if you're above base, or use a variable ND. The calculator shows the closest filter and the gap — a gap under ⅓ stop is usually fine.
