Aperture Explained

What f-stops mean, why the numbers are 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4…, how aperture controls light and depth of field — and the best f-stop for every genre.

Aperture is the hole in your lens that lets light through. It does exactly two things: controls how much light reaches the sensor, and controls how much of the scene is sharp (depth of field). Everything else in photography is built on those two.

What the f-number means

The f-number is focal length ÷ aperture diameter. A 50 mm lens at f/2 has a 25 mm opening; at f/8 it's a 6.25 mm opening. Confusingly, smaller numbers = wider openings = more light. Each full stop doubles the light, and because the opening is a circle (area = πr²), the diameter must multiply by √2 ≈ 1.414 to double the area. That's why the scale looks like this:

What aperture does to your photos

  • Wide open (f/1.2–f/2.8): lots of light, thin depth of field, dreamy backgrounds — but only a sliver of the subject is sharp, and edges may be soft.
  • Middle (f/4–f/8): the sharpest range on most lenses, still decent blur, enough light for most situations.
  • Stopped down (f/11–f/22): maximum depth of field, but light drops fast and diffraction starts softening detail past f/8–f/11.

Aperture FAQ

What does f-stop mean?

The f-number is focal length ÷ aperture diameter. Lower number = wider opening = more light, shallower depth of field.

Why are f-stops 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4?

Each stop doubles the light; area doubles, so diameter multiplies by √2 ≈ 1.414. The scale: 1, 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22, 32.

What's the sharpest aperture?

Usually f/4–f/8 on full frame — after that, diffraction softens pixels. See the diffraction guide for your camera's exact limit.

Does a wider aperture mean better photos?

Not automatically. It means more light and more blur — and less of the scene in focus, plus softer edges wide open.
