Exposure Triangle Explained

Three controls, one result: the amount of light that reaches your sensor. Here's what each one does, how they trade off, and which to change first — with numbers you can verify in the exposure equivalence calculator.

The three corners

ControlWhat it changesCost of overdoing itOne stop =
Aperture (f/)Amount of light + depth of fieldNarrow apertures → diffraction softnessf/2.8 → f/4 → f/5.6 → f/8… (each halves the light)
Shutter speed (s)Amount of light + motion blurSlow speeds → camera shake and subject blur1/500 → 1/250 → 1/125 → 1/60… (each doubles the light)
ISOAmplification of the captured lightHigh ISO → visible noise100 → 200 → 400 → 800 (each doubles sensitivity)

The trade-off, with real numbers

Say your meter says f/4 · 1/250 s · ISO 100. That's an exposure value of EV100 = 12. Every combination below captures the same amount of light:

ApertureShutterISOResult
f/41/250 s100Starting point — balanced
f/2.81/500 s100Shallower DoF, freezes motion
f/5.61/125 s100More DoF, risk of blur
f/41/500 s200Faster shutter, ISO doubles
f/81/60 s100Maximum DoF, needs steady camera

Every row is identically bright. The only differences are aesthetic: depth of field, motion blur, and noise. That's why "correct exposure" is a creative choice, not a number.

Which corner to change first (priority order)

  1. Shutter speed first — set the slowest speed that still freezes your subject (1/focal length handheld, or the subject chart). Motion blur is the one mistake you can't fix.
  2. Aperture second — dial the depth of field your shot needs (f/1.8 portrait vs f/8 landscape), then…
  3. ISO last — raise ISO only to make up the remaining difference. Modern cameras are clean up to ISO 1600–3200; noise beats blur.

Common exposure mistakes

  • "I'll fix it in post." Raising exposure in editing amplifies noise and clips highlights. Better to expose right (or deliberately overexpose by ⅓–⅔ stop for shadows, then recover — the "expose to the right" technique).
  • Chasing shallow DoF everywhere. f/1.2 portraits with drifting focus are soft; the portrait guide shows when to stop down.
  • ISO fear. On a modern camera, ISO 3200 in low light is usually better than a 1/15 s blurry shot at ISO 100.

Exposure triangle FAQ

Does ISO really belong in the "exposure" triangle?

Strictly, exposure is light only (aperture × shutter). ISO is amplification applied after capture. But in practice the three work together to set final brightness, so the triangle is the useful mental model — with the caveat that ISO doesn't add light, it adds noise.

What is "stopping down" and "opening up"?

One stop = doubling or halving the light. Opening up (wider aperture, slower shutter, higher ISO) doubles light; stopping down halves it. The exposure equivalence calculator does the bookkeeping for you.

Why do my photos look different at the same exposure?

Because exposure isn't the whole picture — the character changes with settings: bokeh from aperture, blur from shutter, grain from ISO. Same brightness, different image. That's exactly why the triangle matters.
