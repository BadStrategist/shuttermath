Worked example

Say you're shooting a waterfall at 1/125 s and want silky motion blur. Stack an ND1000 (10 stops): 1/125 × 2¹⁰ = 8.2 seconds — set the calculator to ND1000 and it shows exactly that. The pattern to internalize: every 3 stops ≈ 8× the shutter time (ND8 at 1/125 → ~1 s), every 10 stops ≈ 1000×. That's why ND64 (6 stops) is the "moving water at dusk" filter and ND1000 (10 stops) is the "bright midday" filter. Shooting in aperture priority, the camera meters through the filter, so the calculator's real job is telling you before you compose whether the resulting time is 2 s (nice streaks) or 30 s (cotton-candy water).

ND filter FAQ

Why does shutter time double with every stop?

Each ND stop halves the light reaching the sensor, so the shutter must stay open twice as long to record the same exposure: 1/60 s at 1 stop becomes 1/30 s, 1/15 s, 1/8 s… With a 10-stop ND1000, a 1/60 s base becomes 1/60 × 2¹⁰ = ~17 s.

What ND strength should I buy first?

ND64 (6 stops) or ND1000 (10 stops) are the two most useful. ND64 suits moving water at mid-day; ND1000 is the classic choice for long exposures of 15–30 s in daylight.

Does stacking two filters add their stops?

Yes — optical densities add, so ND8 (3 stops) + ND64 (6 stops) = 9 stops total. Watch for vignetting and color cast when stacking.
